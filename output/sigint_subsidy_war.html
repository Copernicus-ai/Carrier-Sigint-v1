DEVICE SUBSIDY WAR: ARMS RACE OR ARMS TRADE?

Comparative Analysis: T-Mobile · Verizon · AT&T  |  FY2023–FY2025  |  SIGINT Intelligence Platform  |  April 2026
SIGINT VERDICT: T-Mobile is winning the subsidy war — not by outspending rivals, but by converting each dollar of equipment loss into 4–6× more net subscribers than Verizon. At $1,611 per net phone add vs. Verizon's $9,685, T-Mobile's promotional machine is the most capital-efficient in U.S. wireless. Meanwhile Verizon's balance sheet — stretched to 2.78× EBITDA after the Frontier acquisition — limits its ability to respond. AT&T quietly runs the tightest churn and the most balanced subsidy posture of the three.

THE ANALYST ROUNDTABLE

The Verge × Deutsche Bank × Goldman Sachs — Simulated Roundtable, April 2026

THE VERGE ROUNDTABLE — "WHO'S REALLY WINNING THE DEVICE SUBSIDY WAR?"
Published April 2026 | The Verge Technology & Business Desk


PARTICIPANTS


MODERATOR Alex Nguyen — Senior Technology Reporter, The Verge
ANALYST 1 Marcus Behr — Managing Director, Telecom Equity Research,
Deutsche Bank ("TMUS Bull")
ANALYST 2 Diana Rhys — Managing Director, TMT Research,
Goldman Sachs ("VZ/Quality Bull")


PART I — SETTING THE STAGE


ALEX: Let's start with the basics. Every carrier is dangling a
free phone at you right now. Samsung Galaxy S25, iPhone 16 —
carriers are practically giving them away. I want to know
who is actually winning this spending contest, and who is
writing checks they can't cash. Marcus, you cover T-Mobile.
Make the case.

MARCUS: I'll make it simply. In 2025, T-Mobile's equipment gross loss —
the real, net cost of subsidizing devices after recovering what
customers pay — was $5.3 billion. That sounds like a lot of
money. It is a lot of money. But for that $5.3 billion, they
acquired 3.3 million net new postpaid phone subscribers. The
math is $1,611 per net new phone customer. Now ask Diana what
Verizon paid per net add.

DIANA: I'll answer that because the framing is wrong. Verizon's net
phone additions were 362,000 in 2025, and their equipment loss
was $3.5 billion — which your math calls $9,685 per add. But
that is an entirely misleading number. Verizon's device subsidy
is not primarily an acquisition weapon. Eighty percent of that
spend is targeted upgrade offers for existing postpaid accounts —
customers already paying $147 per month in ARPA. Verizon is
protecting the most lucrative customer base in U.S. wireless.
You don't measure retention spend by new customer count.

MARCUS: Diana, with respect, that distinction has been the Verizon
investor talking point for five years. Meanwhile, T-Mobile grew
postpaid phone subscribers from 71 million to 78 million between
2022 and 2025 — largely at Verizon's expense. Those are real
people who switched. That's real ARPU walking out the door.


PART II — THE BALANCE SHEET QUESTION


ALEX: Let's talk debt, because subsidies are ultimately financed
somehow. All three carriers are running net debt above $80
billion. Who has the healthiest balance sheet to keep this
game going?

DIANA: This is where I give T-Mobile full credit — reluctantly.
Their net leverage is the tightest in the group at 2.4x
EBITDA. They've paid down the Sprint acquisition debt faster
than most analysts expected. And their adjusted free cash flow
hit $18 billion in 2025 — up 32% in two years. If there is
a carrier that can sustain or even accelerate device subsidies
without stressing its balance sheet, it is T-Mobile. I will
not dispute that.

MARCUS: Thank you. And to your own thesis — Verizon closed the Frontier
acquisition in September 2024 for $28.5 billion, which pushed
their gross debt past $158 billion. Cash from operations is
strong, but free cash flow is roughly flat at $20 billion. Their
leverage is now 2.8x. That's not dangerous territory, but it
means the room to escalate subsidy intensity is more constrained
than it was two years ago. If a price war breaks out — if Apple
launches a promotion cycle on iPhone 17 and every carrier tries
to out-offer the other — Verizon cannot respond as aggressively
without moving the credit needle.

DIANA: I'll grant the Frontier point. But let's not ignore what
Frontier actually bought them: a fiber footprint that is the
strategic hedge against wireless-only disintermediation.
Verizon's convergence story — wireless plus fiber in the same
bill — is what drives ARPA and reduces churn. Their residential
FWA broadband base grew 25% to 3.4 million subscribers in 2025.
Convergence is Verizon's long game. You cannot value that
on a quarterly net-adds scorecard.


PART III — AT&T: THE SILENT COMPETITOR


ALEX: Neither of you has said much about AT&T. They added 1.55
million postpaid phone customers in 2025 — far more than
Verizon — while running the tightest churn in the group at
0.90%. What is AT&T doing right that doesn't get enough credit?

DIANA: AT&T is running the most operationally disciplined subsidy
program of the three. Their estimated equipment gross loss is
in the $4 billion range — similar in absolute terms to
T-Mobile, but AT&T is converting that into 1.55 million adds
while simultaneously improving their service EBITDA margin
to 51%. And critically: their postpaid phone churn was the
best of the three heading into 2025. What AT&T figured out —
and T-Mobile has not yet fully absorbed — is that subsidy
and churn management are not separate tactics. You use device
promotions to lock in subscribers on multi-year device payment
plans. The phone becomes the retention instrument.

MARCUS: I agree AT&T deserves credit for execution. But their gross
adds trajectory is declining — 1.55 million in 2025 versus
1.74 million in 2023. That is a company managing a plateau,
not accelerating. And their net debt moved from $126 billion
to $117 billion over the period — improvement, yes, but they
still carry the most constrained balance sheet of the three
relative to their EBITDA base when you factor in pension
obligations and DIRECTV-related contingencies.

ALEX: If you had to rank the three on "subsidy efficiency" — who
is getting the most strategic value per dollar of equipment
loss — how would you each rank them?

MARCUS: T-Mobile first, AT&T second, Verizon third. And I want to
add something that I think the annual Verizon number obscures.
Verizon's Consumer segment lost 414,000 postpaid phone subscribers
in the first three quarters of 2025. That is not retention. That
is a bleed. They then spent their way to 616,000 net adds in Q4
alone — Black Friday, iPhone deals, the works — to rescue the
annual number to a positive 362,000. So the strategy Diana is
defending produced nine months of subscriber losses followed by
a panic promotion in Q4. That is not a disciplined premium
retention posture. That is reactive spending to cover a bad year.

DIANA: My ranking: Verizon first, T-Mobile second, AT&T third —
but the question is wrong. "Efficiency" depends entirely on
what you're optimizing. If your goal is market share at any
cost, T-Mobile wins. If your goal is long-term service
revenue per customer, Verizon wins. And Marcus — the Q4
recovery you are calling "panic spending" added 616,000
postpaid phone customers in a single quarter. That is more
than AT&T did in all of Q3 and Q4 combined. You don't panic
your way to 616,000 net adds. You execute. The Q1–Q3 softness
was a deliberate pull-back on unprofitable promotions earlier
in the year. Verizon chose margin over volume for three
quarters, then activated at the highest-value moment in the
calendar. That is not reactive. That is sequencing.

MARCUS: Diana, "deliberate pull-back" is a polite way of saying they
lost the switching battle for three quarters and bought their
way back in Q4. We can argue about intention all we want —
the subscriber voted with their SIM card. And I will add one
more number that does not get enough attention: bad debt.
Subsidies do not just cost you in equipment gross loss —
they cost you again when customers default on their device
payment plans. AT&T's provision for credit losses hit
$2.3 billion in 2025, which is 3.4 cents of every service
revenue dollar gone to bad debt. That is the highest ratio
of the three. T-Mobile's bad debt is rising — up 53% over
two years to $1.4 billion — which is the cost of aggressive
EIP financing behind all those net adds. The point is: the
true cost of subsidy is equipment loss plus bad debt plus
churn-driven re-acquisition. When you add it all up, the gap
between T-Mobile and Verizon narrows. T-Mobile still wins
on efficiency, but by less than the headline numbers suggest.

DIANA: Agreed on bad debt as a hidden cost — and notice that
Verizon's credit loss provision has been essentially flat for
three years at $2.3 billion despite growing its receivables
base substantially through Frontier. Their credit underwriting
is tighter. That is a function of the premium customer profile
they are defending.


PART IV — THE 12-MONTH OUTLOOK


ALEX: Last question. Apple iPhone 17 is expected later this year.
Analysts are predicting the biggest upgrade cycle in four
years. What does that do to the subsidy dynamic?

MARCUS: It is a red-letter event for T-Mobile. They have 78 million
postpaid customers, a significant share of whom are on older
devices. The upgrade cycle means promotional spending goes up
for everyone — but T-Mobile's $17 billion of adjusted free cash
flow and 2.4x leverage gives them the most room to be
aggressive. I would expect them to offer the most compelling
trade-in deals and to lead the industry in net adds through
Q3 and Q4 of this year. This is precisely when the balance
sheet health we talked about becomes the competitive weapon.

DIANA: Marcus is right that T-Mobile has room to be aggressive.
Where we disagree is on whether aggressive is smart. Verizon
will participate in the upgrade cycle but they will be
disciplined — offering promotions targeted at high-ARPA
multi-line accounts, not entry-level switchers. Their goal
will be to deepen the MyPlan ecosystem and improve
connections-per-account. I expect Verizon's ARPA to cross
$150 by end of 2026, which is the number that makes their
whole thesis re-rate. AT&T will do what AT&T does: execute
quietly and let the churn rate do the talking.

ALEX: Marcus, Diana, thank you both. The phones are free.
The debate, apparently, is not.
